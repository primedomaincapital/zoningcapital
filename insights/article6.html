How Long Entitlement Takes, by Project Type

An accessory dwelling unit can clear in sixty days. A subdivision can take three years. The project type decides the calendar — and the calendar decides the carry cost.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Two developers filed in the same city in the same month. One was building an accessory dwelling unit in a backyard. The other was subdividing forty acres into a hundred and twenty lots. The first broke ground in the spring. The second was still in hearings two years later, and the loan on the land had cost more by then than the engineering. They were not in different markets. They were in different project types, and the project type is what decides the calendar. Anybody who underwrites land without underwriting the calendar is underwriting half the deal.

The fastest entitlement in American housing is the accessory dwelling unit, and in California it is fast by law. State law requires a local agency to approve or deny a complete ADU application within 60 days, ministerially — no hearing, no discretionary review, no neighbor’s veto. The reform wave that produced that rule is now national in scale: the American Enterprise Institute estimates that ten state laws passed in 2026 could add about 103,000 homes a year, and roughly half of them are ADUs — California’s AB 956, which loosens ADU rules, accounts for an estimated 48,000 a year, Washington’s SB 6026, which allows homes in commercially zoned areas, for about 37,000, and Idaho’s SB 1352 on smaller lots for an estimated 5,000. An ADU is the project type where the entitlement is a clock, not a negotiation, and the clock runs in weeks.

The next step up is the duplex and the lot split, and the calendar stretches from weeks to months. California’s SB 9, which allows a homeowner to split a single-family lot and build up to two units on each half, is also ministerial in the jurisdictions that have implemented it as written — but the implementation varies, and the variance is the story. A lot split that takes three months in one city can take nine in the next, because the ministerial process still requires a survey, a parcel map, utility clearances, and a planning department that has staffed the desk. The pattern holds across the small-project types: the law sets the ceiling on the delay, and the city’s capacity sets the floor. Ask three entitlement lawyers for the timeline on a duplex and you get three calendars — but they all fall inside a band of three to six months, and none of them involves a public hearing.

The hearing is where the calendar breaks. A rezoning — changing what the land is allowed to be — is a legislative act, and a legislative act takes six to eighteen months in most jurisdictions: application, staff report, planning commission hearing, city council hearing, and the appeal window after each one. A subdivision, which adds a tentative map, an environmental review, and infrastructure conditions to the rezoning, takes twelve to twenty-four months at best, and three years is not unusual when the environmental review is a full impact report rather than a mitigated negative declaration. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which became law in July 2026, seeks to cut the regulatory barriers that slow construction and offers grants to states and local governments that encourage new development — but a federal grant does not shorten a city council’s calendar. It shortens the list of things the council can require before it votes.

The stakes behind the calendar are measured in millions of homes. Realtor.com’s 2026 supply-gap report put the national deficit at about 4.03 million homes in 2025, up from 3.8 million in 2024. Freddie Mac estimated roughly 3.7 million units as of late 2024, Zillow about 4.3 million. In 2025 roughly 1.41 million new households formed while about 1.36 million housing starts were recorded — an annual deficit near 50,000 homes even before the accumulated backlog. Realtor.com estimated that even if construction ran 50% above the 2025 pace and pent-up demand fully dissipated, eliminating the shortage would take about seven years. The country built about 418,000 starter homes a year in the 1970s, roughly a third of all starts, and only about 65,000 by 2020, under 10%. Buyers did not stop wanting small homes. Land rules made them impossible to deliver at a price that covered the dirt — and the dirt’s price includes the years spent waiting for permission to build on it.

Minneapolis is the test case reformers cite and skeptics pick apart, and both sides are partly right. In 2019 the city abolished single-family-only zoning, allowing up to three units on any residential lot. From 2017 to 2022 it grew its housing stock by about 12% while rents rose just 1%; the rest of Minnesota added about 4% supply and saw rents climb 14%. But the apartment reforms, not the duplex rule, did the heavy lifting: of roughly 21,000 units permitted in Minneapolis in that period, about 87% were in buildings of 20 or more units, while only about 1% came from the two-to-four-unit buildings that ending single-family zoning was meant to produce. A 2025 Minneapolis Fed analysis cautioned that part of the divergence may reflect softening demand rather than supply alone. Joe Gyourko’s March 2026 Brookings primer makes the same point from the other direction: do not expect a large or immediate increase in housing supply from upzoning. Corridor upzoning and eliminating parking minimums mattered more than the headline duplex change — and the projects that moved fastest were the ones the new rules made ministerial, not the ones they merely made legal.

The counterargument is the one every planning director makes, and it is not a dodge: speed is not the same as certainty, and a fast denial is worse than a slow approval. A ministerial process that approves an ADU in sixty days also denies one in sixty days, and the denial is final in a way a hearing’s continuance is not. The streamlining laws have hooks of their own — objective standards, fire access, utility capacity, historic review — and a project that trips one of them can spend longer in the streamlined process than it would have spent in the old one, because the streamlined process has no hearing in which to argue the exception. Massachusetts voters showed the other side of the same coin on November 3, 2026, rejecting Question 7 — a statewide 5,000-square-foot minimum lot for single-family homes with public water and sewer — by 50.6% to 49.4%, even with more than 1.2 million voters in favor. The map can be redrawn in the statehouse and still lose at the lot line, and the lot line is where the calendar is set.

Underwrite the calendar the way you underwrite the rate. A land loan at 10% on a project that takes three years to entitle costs 30% before the first lot sells; the same loan on a sixty-day ADU costs less than 2%. The project type decides which of those numbers you are paying, and the decision is made the day the parcel is bought, not the day the application is filed. Buy the ADU and the calendar is a clock. Buy the subdivision and the calendar is a negotiation, a hearing, an appeal, and an environmental report — and the carry cost of all four is the real price of the permission.
