The Starter Home Lost at the Lot Line

Ten state laws could add 103,000 homes a year. Massachusetts voters just rejected the smallest one. Reform is winning in statehouses and losing where the price of a house is actually set.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

On November 3, 2026, Massachusetts voters had a chance to shrink the lot it takes to build a single-family home, and they said no by the thinnest of margins. Question 7 would have set a statewide minimum of 5,000 square feet for single-family lots that have public water and sewer and at least 50 feet of frontage. It failed 50.6% to 49.4%, even with more than 1.2 million voters in favor. The same week, the American Enterprise Institute told a housing conference in Washington that ten state laws passed this year could add an estimated 103,000 homes a year nationally. Reform is now popular in the abstract and moving through statehouses. It is still losing at the lot line, which is where the cost of a starter home is actually set.

The math behind Question 7 was simple. A National Zoning Atlas finding, cited by Realtor.com, shows that more than half of Massachusetts municipalities require roughly 40,000 square feet — about an acre — to build one home. The state’s median listing price is $699,990, about $250,000 above the national median of $415,000. The Homebuilders and Remodelers Association of Massachusetts puts the state’s shortage at 220,000 homes. A 5,000-square-foot minimum would not have built a single house by itself. It would have made it legal to build a small house on a small lot, which in most of the state is currently illegal. Voters declined, by 1.2 percentage points, to make it legal.

Nationally, the trend runs the same direction, and the numbers are large. Roughly half of the AEI-projected 103,000 homes a year are accessory dwelling units: California’s AB 956, which loosens ADU rules, accounts for an estimated 48,000 a year, Washington’s SB 6026, which allows homes in commercially zoned areas, for about 37,000, and Idaho’s SB 1352 on smaller lots for an estimated 5,000. In July, the bipartisan 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law, seeking to cut the regulatory barriers that slow construction and offering grants to states and local governments that encourage new development. The country built about 418,000 starter homes a year in the 1970s, roughly a third of all starts, and only about 65,000 by 2020, under 10%. Buyers did not stop wanting small homes. Land rules made them impossible to deliver at a price that covered the dirt.

The shortage behind all of it is measured in millions, and the estimates disagree only about how many millions. Realtor.com’s 2026 supply-gap report put the deficit at about 4.03 million homes in 2025, up from 3.8 million in 2024. Freddie Mac estimated roughly 3.7 million units as of late 2024, Zillow about 4.3 million. In 2025 roughly 1.41 million new households formed while about 1.36 million housing starts were recorded — an annual deficit near 50,000 homes even before the accumulated backlog. Realtor.com estimated that even if construction ran 50% above the 2025 pace and pent-up demand fully dissipated, eliminating the shortage would take about seven years.

Minneapolis is the test case reformers cite and skeptics pick apart, and both sides are partly right. In 2019 the city abolished single-family-only zoning, allowing up to three units on any residential lot. From 2017 to 2022 it grew its housing stock by about 12% while rents rose just 1%; the rest of Minnesota added about 4% supply and saw rents climb 14%. But the apartment reforms, not the duplex rule, did the heavy lifting: of roughly 21,000 units permitted in Minneapolis in that period, about 87% were in buildings of 20 or more units, while only about 1% came from the two-to-four-unit buildings that ending single-family zoning was meant to produce. A 2025 Minneapolis Fed analysis cautioned that part of the divergence may reflect softening demand rather than supply alone. Joe Gyourko’s March 2026 Brookings primer makes the same point from the other direction: do not expect a large or immediate increase in housing supply from upzoning. Corridor upzoning and eliminating parking minimums mattered more than the headline duplex change.

That is the implication for anyone whose money is in land rather than in legislation. Entitlement value does not accrue to every parcel when a state upzones. It accrues to the parcels that can move first — the ones near a corridor, the ones where an ADU or a commercial conversion is already legal, the ones whose owners read the new map and act on it before the market reprices the whole street. Massachusetts just showed that the map can be redrawn in the statehouse and still lose at the lot line. The parcels that win are the ones where the lot line already moved.
