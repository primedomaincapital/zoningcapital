What Entitlement Lending Costs: Rates, Fees, and the Draw Schedule

The rate on an entitlement loan is the least interesting number in the term sheet. The fees, the draw schedule, and the equity behind it decide what the money actually costs.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The developer signed the term sheet on a Tuesday and did the arithmetic on the back of the page on Wednesday. The rate said 10%. The origination fee was two points. The draws would come in six tranches, each one released only after an inspector signed off on the work, and the interest would run on the drawn balance from the day each tranche landed. By the time the entitlement was finished and the lots were ready to sell, the all-in cost of the money was closer to 14% a year — and the developer had not misread anything. The rate was never the cost. The rate was the headline.

Land is the most expensive thing in commercial real estate to borrow against, and the reason is simple: it earns nothing while you own it. A lender underwriting an entitled parcel is underwriting a permission as well as a place, and the permission can be delayed, appealed, or voted down. The pricing reflects that. As of September 2026, land loans were running 7.5% to 11.5% according to BestLoanUSA’s commercial rate survey, 9% to 13% in the Real Estate Journal’s land development financing guide, and 9% to 14%, usually interest-only, in Financial Compound’s land loan terms. The range is wide because the risk is wide: a raw parcel with no approvals and no utilities is a different loan from an entitled infill site with a final map, and the market prices the difference. A lender offering 90% of value on unentitled land is not a bargain. It is a warning.

Loan-to-value is where the entitlement shows up first. Raw land typically borrows 40% to 55% of value. Entitled land — zoning, density, and use permits in place, tentative or final map recorded — borrows 55% to 70%. An approved infill site can reach 60% to 75%. The difference between raw and entitled is ten to fifteen points of proceeds, which is the market’s price for the regulatory risk the entitlement retired. Hard money lenders, who close fastest, typically lend 65% to 70% of cost on raw land over 12 to 24 months at 10% to 13%, and they close in 10 to 21 days — which matters when a seller has competing offers or a contract has a tight diligence window. The bank will lend cheaper. The bank will also take longer, ask for more, and walk away if the entitlement slips.

The capital stack above the land loan is where the true cost hides. A bridge loan runs about 6.25% to 8.25% at 65% to 80% loan-to-value over 12 to 36 months; a construction loan 6.75% to 10.5% at 65% to 80% of cost over 12 to 24 months. Mezzanine debt, which sits between the senior mortgage and the equity and lets a sponsor reach 75% to 90% combined leverage, runs 11% to 16% over two to seven years. The equity behind it all demands 20% to 35% preferred returns, with promote structures that allocate 60% to 80% of the profit to the equity if the project performs. The Real Estate Journal’s worked example makes the arithmetic concrete: a 120-lot subdivision with a $3,200,000 land acquisition and a $6,096,500 infrastructure budget, funded with a senior land loan at 60% to 70% of acquisition cost, a senior development loan at 65% to 75% of infrastructure cost, developer equity for the land gap, and joint venture equity for the infrastructure gap, carries a blended cost of capital of approximately 14% to 17% a year. The land loan’s rate was the cheapest line in the stack. It was also the smallest.

The draw schedule is the part of the deal that decides whether the project survives its own timeline. Entitlement and development money does not arrive in a lump sum at closing. It releases against completed milestones — typically four to six draws tied to grading, utilities, foundation, and framing — and each draw needs an inspection, a sign-off, and a lender’s approval before the next tranche moves. Interest runs only on what has been drawn, which is the good news, and the bad news is that the schedule is the lender’s control mechanism: a missed milestone is a missed draw, and a missed draw is a project that stops while the carry cost keeps running. A bridge-to-construction structure, one loan covering the land purchase and the vertical build and drawn in stages, avoids a second closing and a second set of fees, commonly over 18 to 24 months at 65% to 70% of the combined acquisition-plus-construction budget. The saving is real. So is the concentration: one lender, one set of conditions, one point of failure.

The fees beyond the rate are where the term sheet earns its keep, and they are the lines most borrowers read last. An origination fee of one to two points is common on hard money land loans, charged at closing on the full commitment whether or not it is ever drawn. An exit fee, usually a point or two, is charged when the loan is repaid — which means the borrower pays to leave as well as to enter. An extension fee applies when the entitlement runs past the loan’s term, which it often does: a twelve-month loan on an eighteen-month entitlement is a loan that will need an extension, and the extension is priced when the borrower has the least leverage, because the alternative is a default on the day the permission is closest to landing. A prepayment penalty, where one exists, locks the borrower into the full term even if the entitlement finishes early. None of these fees appears in the rate. All of them appear in the cost, and the cost is the number that decides whether the project works.

The benchmarks behind all of it moved in 2026, and the direction was not helpful. The September 2026 survey put the 10-year Treasury at 5.18%, SOFR at 3.88%, and prime at 7% — a bank bridge loan priced at SOFR plus 2.25% to 3.25% lands at 6.25% to 7.25%, and a debt fund bridge at SOFR plus 2.75% to 4.25% lands at 6.75% to 8.25%. The SBA 504 program, for owner-occupied projects, runs 6.25% to 7.25% at up to 90% loan-to-value over 10 to 25 years — the cheapest money in the survey, and the least available to a speculative entitlement play, because the SBA does not finance a permission. It finances a business.

The counterargument is the one every developer makes when the rate card looks ugly, and it is not wrong: expensive money that closes in two weeks beats cheap money that never closes. The entitlement clock is the real cost. A parcel under contract with a 60-day diligence window, a seller with three backup offers, and a planning commission hearing in ninety days does not need the lowest rate. It needs a lender that can fund before the window closes, and a hard money loan at 10% to 13% that closes in 10 to 21 days is worth more than a bank loan at 7% that takes ninety days to underwrite and then declines. The rate is a line item. The lost parcel is the whole project.

That is the right way to read the term sheet, and it is also the reason the term sheet has to be read whole. Before signing, ask three questions. First, what is the all-in cost per month of carry — rate plus fees plus the equity’s preferred return, divided by the months the entitlement will take — because a 10% rate on a three-year entitlement is a 30% cost before the first lot sells. Second, how do the draws work: how many, tied to what milestones, released on whose sign-off, and what happens to the schedule when a milestone slips by a quarter? Third, what is the exit: does the loan convert into construction financing, is there a pre-arranged refinance into a long-term loan once the lots are ready, or does the whole stack come due the month the entitlement lands? The developer who signed on Tuesday and did the arithmetic on Wednesday got it right. The rate was the least interesting number in the term sheet. The cost was the calendar, and the calendar was the entitlement.
