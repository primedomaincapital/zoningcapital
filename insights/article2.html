Lending Against a Permission, Not Just a Parcel

Entitled land borrows differently from raw land, because the lender is underwriting a permission as well as a place.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

A lender looking at raw land and a lender looking at entitled land are looking at two different loans, even when the dirt is identical. The first loan is secured by a parcel whose highest use is still a hope. The second is secured by a parcel whose highest use is already a permission, with a subdivision map, utility commitments, and final plans to prove it. The interest rate, the loan-to-value, the draw schedule, and the exit all change at the moment the permission arrives — which is why entitlement lending is a discipline of its own, not a smaller version of construction lending.

The reason starts with what a finished lot represents in the cost of a home. In the national builder survey conducted in the fall of 2024, the finished lot averaged 13.7% of a new single-family home's sales price, against 64.4% for construction and 11.0% for builder profit. That 13.7% is the land stake, but it understates the lot's importance: without an entitled, build-ready lot, the other 86.3% cannot be spent at all. A lender who funds raw land is therefore funding the riskiest seventh of the project first, before any of the value-creating work has been done. A lender who funds entitled land is funding a parcel a builder can start on within weeks.

Entitlement risk is, above all, time risk and binary risk. Practitioner guides describe entitlement timelines from three to twelve months for simpler projects to a month to a few years depending on scope, with studies, hearings, and overlapping city, county, and state jurisdictions each capable of adding delay. A land development firm working in the Southwest puts its own market at six months to one year, and sometimes longer. For a borrower, that is carrying cost: interest, taxes, and option payments accruing while no revenue exists. For a lender, it is the risk that the permission never arrives, arrives in a reduced form — fewer units, a different use, a condition that breaks the pro forma — or arrives after the market it was designed for has moved.

That is why entitled land is valued on a ladder, and why lenders price each rung differently. The same Southwest firm values raw land at approximately 60% of its potential total economic value, tentatively approved land at approximately 80%, and fully entitled land at 100%, with finished lots commanding about a 10% premium over fully entitled land. A lender can read that ladder as a collateral ladder: each approval step does not merely add value, it removes a category of risk — rezoning risk, then site-plan risk, then utility risk — and each removal justifies a larger advance, a lower rate, or a longer term. Conversely, a loan made against raw land at a fully entitled valuation is over-advanced on day one, and no construction draw schedule can fix that.

The cost side of the ladder matters to underwriting as well. Third-party entitlement costs of $25,000 to over $100,000 to reach tentative approval, and a further $100,000 to over $250,000 to reach final approval, are not soft costs to be waved through. They are the budget for the permission itself, and they must be funded, staged, and verified like any other draw: studies delivered, surveys recorded, utility letters received, fees paid. A lender who does not verify each deliverable is funding a promise rather than a permission. A borrower who cannot produce them has not yet earned the next rung.

Regulation adds a second, national layer to the same underwriting. The national builder association estimates that government regulation accounts for 26.4% of the average final price of a new home — $131,734 on a $499,500 home in its 2026 figures — including land dedicated to government, builder fees, design and landscaping standards, and the zoning application itself. Not all of that is entitlement, and local figures vary widely, but the direction is clear: a material share of what a buyer pays for a new home is the cost of getting permission to build it. A lender who ignores that share will misprice both the loan and the collateral, because the regulatory cost is incurred before the first saleable home exists.

The pipeline data sharpens the point. In September 2026, housing starts ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1,221,000, down 5.2% from August and 7.4% from a year earlier, while building permits ran at 1,400,000, down 0.2% on the month and 3.0% on the year. Single-family starts were 859,000 and single-family permits 845,000. Permits are the closest public proxy for the entitlement pipeline: they measure projects that have cleared enough approval to be authorized, even if ground has not yet broken. When permits hold up better than starts, the pipeline is still being fed; when both fall together, the constraint is upstream, in the approvals themselves, in financing, or in both. A land lender reads that gap the way a banker reads a loan book: starts are today's revenue, permits are tomorrow's.

The practical underwriting checklist follows from all of this. First, stage: is the parcel raw, tentatively approved, fully entitled, or finished, and what evidence proves it? Second, remainder: what approvals, studies, fees, and utility commitments stand between the current stage and the next, who pays for each, and how long have comparable parcels in the same jurisdiction taken? Third, density: does the current zoning allow the unit count in the pro forma, or does the pro forma assume a rezoning that has not yet happened? Fourth, expiry: do the approvals lapse, and on what date, if construction does not start? A loan that answers those four questions has underwritten a permission. A loan that does not has underwritten a hope, at a permission's price.
