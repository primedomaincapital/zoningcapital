The Land Bank’s Play: Buying Parcels Before the Upzoning Arrives

The land bank buys the parcel the map has not reached yet. The profit is the gap between today’s price and the price after the vote — and the vote is the risk.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The parcel is forty acres of scrub on the edge of a city that is about to run out of room, and the asking price is the price of scrub. The comprehensive plan update is eighteen months away. The corridor study is already circulating. The council member for the district has said, on the record, that the city needs housing on the corridor. Somebody is going to buy this parcel, hold it through the plan update, and sell it as entitled land for three times the price — or watch the plan update go the other way and hold scrub for a decade. That somebody is the land bank, and the decision it makes on this parcel is the whole business.

Land banking is the oldest play in real estate and the least understood, because it looks like doing nothing. The land bank buys land that is cheap because it cannot yet be built on, carries it through the entitlement process, and sells it — or develops it — once the permission exists. The profit is the gap between the price of raw land and the price of entitled land, and the gap is large: raw land typically borrows at 40% to 55% of value, entitled land at 55% to 70%, and the difference in proceeds is the market’s price for the regulatory risk the entitlement retired. The land bank’s return is that price, minus the carry cost of holding the land while the permission is pursued — land loans at 9% to 14%, usually interest-only, over one to five years — and minus the cost of the entitlement itself, which is the engineering, the environmental review, the traffic studies, and the hearings.

The professionals who do this at scale have a name for the discipline, and it is optionality. Walton Global, one of the largest land asset managers in North America, built its business on buying land in the path of growth and holding it through the planning cycle. The public homebuilders learned the same lesson from the other direction: companies including Lennar have moved toward land-light models, controlling land through options and joint ventures rather than owning it outright, so that the balance sheet carries the right to buy rather than the land itself. The option is the purest form of the land bank’s play. A fee buys the right to purchase a parcel at a set price within a set window. If the upzoning arrives, the option is exercised and the parcel is worth multiples of the strike price. If it does not, the option expires and the loss is the fee. The option converts a binary bet on a vote into a priced instrument, and the price of the option is the price of being wrong.

The map that matters is not the zoning map. It is the map of where the zoning map is about to change, and the signals are public if you know where to look. A comprehensive plan update, a corridor study, a transit line, a state law that preempts local rules — each one redraws the boundary between the land that can be built on and the land that cannot, and each one is announced years before it takes effect. The American Enterprise Institute estimates that ten state laws passed in 2026 could add about 103,000 homes a year, and roughly half of them are accessory dwelling units: California’s AB 956, which loosens ADU rules, accounts for an estimated 48,000 a year, Washington’s SB 6026, which allows homes in commercially zoned areas, for about 37,000, and Idaho’s SB 1352 on smaller lots for an estimated 5,000. The parcels that win are the ones near a corridor, the ones where an ADU or a commercial conversion is already legal, the ones whose owners read the new map and act on it before the market reprices the whole street.

Minneapolis is the case study, and it cuts both ways. In 2019 the city abolished single-family-only zoning, allowing up to three units on any residential lot. From 2017 to 2022 it grew its housing stock by about 12% while rents rose just 1%; the rest of Minnesota added about 4% supply and saw rents climb 14%. But the apartment reforms, not the duplex rule, did the heavy lifting: of roughly 21,000 units permitted in Minneapolis in that period, about 87% were in buildings of 20 or more units, while only about 1% came from the two-to-four-unit buildings that ending single-family zoning was meant to produce. A land bank that bought small residential parcels in 2019 betting on the duplex rule bought the wrong parcels. A land bank that bought corridor parcels betting on the apartment reforms bought the right ones. The upzoning lifted the city. It did not lift every parcel equally, and the difference between the two was the difference between reading the law and reading the map.

Massachusetts showed the other side of the bet on November 3, 2026, when voters rejected Question 7 — a statewide 5,000-square-foot minimum lot for single-family homes with public water and sewer and at least 50 feet of frontage — by 50.6% to 49.4%, even with more than 1.2 million voters in favor. The state’s median listing price is $699,990, about $250,000 above the national median of $415,000, and the Homebuilders and Remodelers Association of Massachusetts puts the state’s shortage at 220,000 homes. A land bank that bought small lots in Massachusetts in 2025, betting that the vote would pass and the lots would become buildable, bought a position that lost on election night. The map can be redrawn in the statehouse and still lose at the lot line, and the lot line is where the land bank’s money sits.

The counterargument is the one every skeptic makes, and it is the reason land banking has a reputation it does not deserve: buying before the vote is speculation, not investment. The market prices the probability in, and the price of the parcel already reflects the chance the upzoning arrives — so the land bank is not buying an insight, it is buying a lottery ticket at a price the market set. The charge has force, and it is the reason the option structure matters: an option pays a known fee for an unknown outcome, while an outright purchase pays the full price for the same unknown outcome and carries the loan, the taxes, and the entitlement cost while it waits. The land bank that buys outright is speculating. The land bank that buys an option is underwriting, and the difference between the two is the difference between a bet and a business.

The discipline, then, is three rules. First, buy the parcel that works even if the vote fails — the one with a use today, a tenant, a fallback entitlement, a price that does not require the upzoning to justify it. Second, pay for the option, not the land, whenever the vote is the whole thesis — because the fee is the maximum loss, and the maximum loss is the only number that matters when the vote goes the other way. Third, read the map, not the law: the corridor, the transit line, and the apartment reform are what move the price, and the headline duplex change is what moves the press release. The forty acres of scrub on the edge of the city is still forty acres of scrub. The question is not whether the city will grow toward it. The question is whether the parcel can pay for the wait, and whether the wait is priced into the fee — because the land bank that answers both questions before it buys is the one still holding the parcel when the map finally arrives.
