Reform Rewards the Parcels That Can Move First

Upzoning does not lift every parcel equally. It lifts the ones whose owners can read the new map and act on it.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

When a city upzones — when it allows more homes on the same land, by right or by a simpler approval — the headlines usually describe a single event: the map changed. The money, however, moves in at least three waves, and only the owners who understand all three capture the gain. The first wave is expectation: prices and plans move when a reform is announced, sometimes years before it takes effect. The second is permission: the parcels that can actually use the new rules, without a discretionary fight, gain a real, buildable advantage. The third is production: homes get built, and the neighborhoods that build fastest see supply, rents, and land values adjust again. Reform rewards the parcels that can move first, not merely the parcels that were rezoned.

Auckland, New Zealand, is the cleanest large-scale test of that sequence. In 2016 the city upzoned approximately three-quarters of its residential land, allowing medium- and high-density housing in areas previously limited to low density. Permits for new dwellings subsequently reached record highs. A synthetic-control study from the University of Auckland's Economic Policy Centre, published as Working Paper No. 017 in September 2025, estimates that the upzoning approximately doubled new dwelling permits per capita within five years of the reform becoming operational, and that seven years on, cumulative permits exceeded the no-reform counterfactual by approximately 52,200 — about 46% of the 112,300 permits issued over the period. The study's point is not that every upzoned parcel was built on. It is that the aggregate effect was large, measurable, and concentrated where the new rules made building feasible, not merely legal in theory.

That distinction — feasible versus merely legal — is the central lesson for landowners and investors. Before Auckland's reform, the city already had roughly 350,000 units of zoned capacity on paper, yet much of it required redevelopment that was not economically feasible at prevailing prices and construction costs. The upzoning worked because it created feasible capacity: projects developers could profitably build right away, mostly attached dwellings such as townhomes and apartments, mostly inside the urban core. A zoning change that adds capacity nobody can profitably use adds nothing. A zoning change that turns a marginal project into a profitable one changes the land value overnight, because the land can now support a product the market will actually pay to build.

The same logic runs in the other direction, and the counterexamples matter. The research literature is mixed precisely because local conditions differ: upzoning coincided with increased construction in Auckland, Houston, New York, Portland, São Paulo, Wellington, and Zurich in various studies, but did not coincide with a construction increase in Minneapolis or San Jose in others. In Minneapolis, quarterly building permits did not exceed 500 in any quarter from the start of 2023, and averaged barely over 100 in 2024, in part because a legal challenge halted implementation of the city's 2040 plan from September 2023 to May 2024 and infused the market with uncertainty. A reform that is legally uncertain is not yet a reform for capital purposes. Developers do not fund lawsuits; they fund permissions.

By-right versus discretionary approval is where that uncertainty is decided, parcel by parcel. By-right — also called ministerial — approval means a project that meets a fixed set of objective standards must be approved, without a discretionary hearing or a political vote. Discretionary approval means a project can meet every written standard and still be denied, delayed, or conditioned into unprofitability by a board, a council, or a neighborhood process. California's accessory dwelling unit reforms illustrate the by-right effect at scale: after 26 amendments between 2016 and 2024, accessory units became allowed by right on all residentially zoned parcels, reviewed within 60 days through a streamlined ministerial process, and their share of new housing construction rose from less than 1% before 2017 to approximately 20%, with more than 23,000 accessory units legally completed in 2023 alone. The units did not become popular because tastes changed. They became buildable because the approval stopped being a gamble.

For a landowner, the practical question after any reform is therefore narrow and specific. First, does the new rule apply to this parcel, or only to a district, a corridor, or a parcel size the owner does not hold? Second, is the new capacity by-right or discretionary, and if discretionary, what has this jurisdiction actually approved, at what density, and after how long? Third, is the new capacity feasible — does the unit count, the height, the parking, and the fee structure produce a project a builder can finance at today's construction costs and rents? Fourth, what is the window: does the reform include an expiry, a sunset, a legal challenge, or a political reversal risk that rewards moving now rather than holding for a better market? Parcels that answer all four questions well are the ones whose values move first. Parcels that answer only the first are the ones whose owners wait, and wonder why the map changed but their price did not.

The national backdrop makes the stakes larger than any single city. Freddie Mac estimated the national housing shortage at 3.7 million units as of the third quarter of 2024, down slightly from 3.8 million in the fourth quarter of 2020 despite 5.8 million additional housing units added in between — demand, in other words, outran even a large building program. Other published estimates range widely depending on method, from about one million to ten million, which is itself a warning: no single shortage number should be quoted as if it were a measurement rather than a model. What the range does establish is direction. The country is short of homes in the places people want to live, and the constraint that binds most often is not land, labor, or lumber in the abstract. It is the permission to build, on the parcel, at the density, in the time frame a project needs.

That is why zoning reform is, for capital, a parcel-selection discipline rather than a policy debate. The reform is the tide. The parcel — its zone, its approval path, its feasibility, its timing — is the boat. Owners who can read a new zoning map the week it is published, verify the approval path in writing, and fund the first feasible project while competitors are still reading the headlines, capture the first wave of value. Owners who wait for the production wave to prove the reform will find that the best parcels have already been repriced. In zoning, as in markets generally, the map is public. The advantage is in moving first on what it actually says.
