Entitlement Is Where Land Value Is Made

Raw land and buildable land are not the same asset. The approvals in between are what turn one into the other.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Two parcels can sit side by side, share the same soil, the same road frontage, and the same view, and be worth entirely different amounts. The difference is not in the ground. It is in the file: what the zoning map allows, what the subdivision map has approved, what the utility letters promise, and what a planning commission has already said yes to. Until those papers exist, a buyer is buying a possibility. After they exist, a buyer is buying a project.

Entitlement is the legal process of gaining the approvals needed to develop a parcel for a specific use. It is complicated, politically nuanced, time-consuming, and expensive, because approvals are required from numerous government agencies at the local, state, and occasionally federal level. That friction is the point of this article, not a footnote to it: the friction is what makes the finished permission scarce, and scarcity is what makes it valuable.

A land development firm working in the Southwest describes the progression in four stages, with a value ladder attached to each. Raw land, often not zoned for its highest economic use and often encumbered by physical defects such as flood zones or poor soils and legal encumbrances such as easements and boundary disputes, is valued at approximately 60% of its potential total economic value. Tentatively approved land — rezoned to the intended use, with a tentatively approved subdivision map, site plan, hydrology and soils studies, survey, tentative utility approval, and an approved traffic study — is valued at approximately 80%, because a significant share of development risk has been mitigated. Fully entitled land, with final plans approved by every authority, utility commitments secured, and improvement plans in place, is valued at 100%. Finished building lots, with all on-site and off-site improvements complete and utilities to the back of the curb, command a further premium: developers will pay about 10% more for them, because a builder can start within weeks of acquiring the parcel.

The costs of climbing that ladder are specific, not vague. Third-party entitlement costs are not uncommonly $25,000 to over $100,000 for the tentative-approval stage alone, covering geologists for hydrology and soils work, civil engineers for utility, site-planning, traffic, and grading work, and legal counsel to navigate the political landscape. Moving from tentative to final approval costs a further $100,000 to over $250,000 in incremental developer cost. Those figures are the price of certainty, and they explain why entitlement is a business in its own right rather than a paperwork step inside a construction budget.

Density is where the ladder steepens. The same Southwest firm gives a Las Vegas illustration: land zoned for one home per acre, rezoned to a designation allowing six to twelve units per acre, may triple in value. The arithmetic is not mysterious. A parcel that can legally hold ten homes instead of one has ten saleable products instead of one, against roughly the same land cost. Rezoning that increases building density increases property values to buyers and sellers alike, because it increases the number of homes the land can produce. That is also why the most valuable zoning work is rarely the most visible: a density change on an unremarkable parcel, quietly approved, can be worth more than a landmark building on a parcel whose density was already fixed.

Time is the other half of the value equation. In that firm's market, entitlement requires between six months and one year, and sometimes longer depending on complexity. Other practitioner guides put the range wider — three to twelve months for simpler projects, and a month to a few years depending on scope, with studies, hearings, and overlapping jurisdictions each adding delay. A developer who underwrites a twelve-month entitlement and gets a thirty-month one has not merely waited longer; the developer has carried land, interest, taxes, and option costs for eighteen extra months, and has watched the market the project was designed for move on. Time, in entitlement, is a cost that compounds.

None of this makes raw land a bad asset. It makes raw land a different asset: cheaper, riskier, and dependent on someone else's future work. The national cost structure of a new home shows the same logic from the builder's side. In the national builder survey conducted in the fall of 2024, construction costs averaged 64.4% of a new single-family home's sales price, finished-lot costs averaged 13.7%, and builder profit averaged 11.0%. The finished lot — land plus its development — is only about a seventh of the final price, yet it is the seventh that determines whether the other six-sevenths can be built at all. A parcel without the right zoning cannot be priced as if it had it, however good the soil or the location.

The implication for anyone buying, selling, or financing land is to price the stage, not just the site. Ask which stage a parcel is at, what remains to reach the next stage, who pays for it, and how long it has taken comparable parcels in the same jurisdiction. A parcel at 60% of its potential value with a clear, funded path to 100% is a different investment from a parcel at 60% with a contested rezoning, a missing utility commitment, or a hearing calendar that keeps slipping. Zoning is the map. Entitlement is the journey across it. The value is made on the journey.
